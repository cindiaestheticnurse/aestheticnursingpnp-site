White-Labeled Injectables & Unapproved Sourcing

What the 2026 FDA warning letters mean for med spas, aesthetic RNs, NPs, PAs and medical directors.

Cindi Vokey, BSN, RN, LNC · Sources Reviewed November 2026

Executive Summary

FDA Enforcement Is Ongoing.
The Primary Issue Is Improper Vendor Sourcing.

Can Your Website Prove Your Sourcing Is Correct?

FDA issued 51 warning letters to telehealth, IV and aesthetic businesses in two 2026 sweeps, and letters were still being posted in November. A recurring finding: a seller's own brand on compounded injectables it did not make. FDA found these violations through websites, product photos and social media, often with no inspection.

01Patients Are Being HarmedCounterfeit toxin hospitalized 11 people; semaglutide errors reached 20 times the dose.
02White Labeling Is a Red FlagA practice name on a vial it did not compound tells FDA the practice made the drug.
03Sourcing Is a Dispenser ProblemFDA's first supply chain (DSCSA) letter to a dispenser went to a med spa in April 2026.
04"Research Use Only" Is Not a Supply ChainRUO disclaimers fail when products are sold for injection.

Company, practice and individual names from the FDA letters have been removed. The goal is awareness and prevention.

White Labeling

Why a Practice Name on a Vial Is a Red Flag

A compliant compounded injectable is labeled by the pharmacy or outsourcing facility that made it, not the practice that buys it. FDA reads a practice logo on a vial as a claim about who manufactured the drug.

A Medical Spa · June 2026"The appearance on a drug product label of a person's name without qualification is a representation" that the named company is the sole manufacturer. 21 CFR 201.1(h)(2)
The 30-Letter SweepBrand names on labels implying the seller compounds the drug, plus "generic," "contains the active ingredient in" and "FDA-approved" claims. FDA · Foley
A Telehealth Company · Oct 2026Website and X photos named the company as compounder, and the site claimed "FDA-Approved Compounded Medications." FDA reviewed only the website and social media.

Why Legitimate Compounders Do Not White Label

A 503A pharmacy compounds for an individual patient and labels with its name and that patient's. A 503B outsourcing facility is FDA-registered and must identify itself on the label. A supplier willing to print your logo is operating outside both pathways.

What the Regulator Sees

Websites, product photos, pinned posts and bios. A branded-vial photo on Instagram is evidence. Every letter requires a written response within 15 business days (FDA).

Unapproved Sources

Where the Drugs Come From

FDA now holds the practice that injects a drug responsible for its source. If you cannot document an authorized source, the product is presumed suspect.

1Supply Chain Law Reaches Med SpasFDA's first DSCSA dispenser letter (April 2026) cited a med spa that administered far more Botox than it bought, plus an unlabeled vial. "Dispenser" includes injecting nurses. Polsinelli · FDA
2Gray-Market NeurotoxinFDA warned 18 websites selling unapproved botulinum toxin in November 2025, citing botulism-like symptoms. FDA · Modern Aesthetics
3"Research Use Only" PeptidesDosing guides, reconstitution calculators and bacteriostatic water show products are meant for injection, including semaglutide, tirzepatide, retatrutide, tesamorelin and PT-141. ArentFox Schiff

Sterile compounding requires a classified cleanroom, not a bench top. A white-labeled vial gives no way to know where it was made.

A Quick Self-Check

Six Questions to Ask Your Practice Today

  1. 01Does every vial we administer show the name of the pharmacy or outsourcing facility that made it, or does it show our name?
  2. 02Can we name, today, the licensed pharmacy or registered outsourcing facility behind every compounded product we use, and verify that licensure?
  3. 03Are our product photos, social posts and sales pages free of "generic," "same as," "same active ingredient" and "FDA-approved pharmacy" language?
  4. 04Are we ordering anything labeled "Research Use Only" or "not for human consumption" for patient use?
  5. 05Does our medical director know exactly where our compounded products come from?
  6. 06Do our policies and procedures include a written vendor-verification and product-sourcing process, and is our team actually following it?

If any answer gives you pause, fix it now, before FDA, your state Board or a patient complaint asks for you.

Compliance Checklist

For Providers and Practice Owners

Sourcing
  • Buy FDA-approved products only from the manufacturer or an authorized distributor; keep invoices.
  • Verify compounders are a licensed 503A pharmacy or FDA-registered 503B facility; keep it on file.
  • Never buy RUO peptides, overseas website product or deeply discounted product.
  • Reconcile units purchased against units administered monthly.
Labeling and Storage
  • Accept only vials naming the maker; refuse your logo on any injectable.
  • Keep products in original labeled packaging; no unlabeled or decanted vials.
Marketing
  • Remove "FDA-approved," "generic," "same as" and "clinically proven" claims for compounded products.
  • Delete photos showing your brand on a vial, including pinned posts and old reels.
  • State that compounded drugs are not FDA-approved and are prescribed after an individual evaluation.
Documentation and Oversight
  • Add sourcing, quarantine, recall and adverse event (MedWatch) procedures to your P&P manual.
  • Document each patient-specific evaluation and dosing teaching.
  • Name who responds to a Form 483 or warning letter within 15 business days.
